2 · Queue — the answer, and the reason
One cost is transient. The other is permanent. Your don't case is real: a room that answers in four minutes is worse to watch than one that answers in one. But that cost is paid by the person watching, and only while they watch — and nobody watches a room, which is the reason rooms exist. You post and go. The never-start cost is paid by the record, forever. Step 9 of your own session is the proof: Otto · did not start · none and Pike · did not start · none, beside four completed words, and step 11 shows 4 local missions in the list — Otto and Pike left no mission at all. Tomorrow the ledger has no trace that they were asked. A warning before the post does not repair a record after it. And the wave is not a defect of the design; it is what the modelled thing looks like. A standup where six people answer at once has never happened. The grid filling in over a few minutes is more legible than six cards resolving simultaneously, because you can read them as they land.
It needs one new state, not three. Waiting is the one, and it is standing: nothing is happening, nothing is wrong, nothing wants you. A roster line, not a card. And it is not even a new phase — RoomAnswer.phase is already "whatever the record says", so this is one more string in a set that already carries six. Next is not a state, it is a position, and it changes without anybody doing anything. Drawing it means the screen mutates while you read it to say which of two identical waits is fractionally sooner. Do not draw it: order the line and let position be position. Cancelled before starting is not a state either — it is a removal. A teammate who never started and never will has nothing to report, so the line loses a name and the count drops. A whole-room cancel is the room's terminal card, which exists.

3 · Does the grid hold At six yes, at ten no — and your own capture shows the mechanism.
Your measurement is right and it is not the constraint. A grid forces equal-height cells, and cell content is of wildly unequal length. In the capture it is not even the answers doing it — every answer is one word — it is opencode/muse-spark-1.3-contributor-free wrapping to two mono lines, which sets the height for two cards that have nothing in them. With real answers it is worse, because prose varies more than model names. One teammate writes two lines, another writes twelve; every row is as tall as its longest cell, and it worsens with width, since a wider row has more chances to contain one long answer. Survivable at six, where a row is three cells and one glance. At ten it is a 4×3 that scrolls, and the grid is then costing you the only thing it was for — seeing the room at once — while still paying the whitespace. Grid to six, list past it, switched on rendered rows rather than member count, since rows are what break. A list gives every answer one column width and its own height, and puts the room in the same shape as the thread.
